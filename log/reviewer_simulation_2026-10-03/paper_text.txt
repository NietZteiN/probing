Chain of Thought Limits Lure Answers in Arithmetic
Anonymous ACL submission
001 Abstract
002 A variable named four can have value 6.
003 We ask whether writing a chain of thought
004 keeps a model from answering 4. We test
005 matched arithmetic problems that differ in
006 one name, across thirteen models and three
007 sets of worked examples. Without a chain,
008 eleven models show a naming effect on accu￾009 racy or lure answers. With a chain, 43 of 44
010 lure contrasts fall within the preregistered ±2-
011 point margin. The exception is OLMo-2-1B￾012 Instruct. On neutral problems, probes read the
013 true value accurately at the step that writes it in
014 9 of 10 tested model–variable pairs; the excep￾015 tion has low probe accuracy at that step. Patch￾016 ing localizes the no-chain effect in Llama-3.2-
017 3B to early layers at the variable’s defining
018 equation. Reduced lure answers accompany
019 accurate value readouts across the tested pairs.
020 Yet the exception can state the correct value
021 and still answer with the lure.
022 1 Introduction
023 In the problem
024 pen=1 + cup, cup=2 + 3; pen=?
025 pen has value 6. Renaming it four leaves the an￾026 swer unchanged:
027 four=1 + cup, cup=2 + 3; four=?
028 The definition says 6; the name suggests 4. We
029 call 4 the lure value. Because it is absent from the
030 equations and differs from every variable’s value,
031 answering 4 points to the name rather than to a
032 copied digit.
033 Misleading identifiers and comments reduce ac￾034 curacy on code tasks (Le et al., 2025; Lam et al.,
035 2025; Le et al., 2026). Accuracy alone cannot
036 locate the error: the model may misread the in￾037 put, compute the wrong value, or answer incor￾038 rectly despite retaining the right value. Arith￾039 metic with named variables gives us a known cor￾040 rect value to compare with the lure. Kudo et al.
(2026) use probes and patching to study when 041
values become available during chain-of-thought 042
(CoT) arithmetic. We add names that disagree 043
with those values. This cue-conflict design, used 044
in Stroop tasks and shape–texture studies (Stroop, 045
1935; Geirhos et al., 2019), gives the name and the 046
computation distinct answers. 047
Writing a chain usually limits lure answers. 048
Without CoT, names affect answers in eleven mod- 049
els. With CoT, almost every lure contrast meets 050
our equivalence bound, although some accuracy 051
effects remain. We then ask what distinguishes 052
the exception. Across the probed model–variable 053
pairs, low lure excess accompanies an accurate 054
value readout at the step that writes it. This is an 055
association; the readout need not drive the answer. 056

108 3 Results
109 3.1 Writing a chain limits lure answers
110 Table 2 shows the naming effects at the main level.
111 Without CoT, a congruent name raises Llama-3.1-
112 8B’s accuracy by +4.8 points, and a misleading
113 name produces +1.2 points of lure excess on the
114 queried variable. Across levels, the largest ef￾115 fects occur where direct-answer accuracy is near
116 chance; the matched baseline prevents those ar￾117 bitrary digit answers from being attributed to the
118 name.
119 With CoT, 43 of 44 lure contrasts meet the
120 equivalence bound. The exception is OLMo-2-1B￾121 Instruct: its intermediate-variable lure excess is
122 +3.2 points [+2.5, +3.9]. Its neutral CoT accuracy
123 is 47%, compared with 77% under letter names.
124 CoT therefore limits the name’s numeric influence
125 without removing every naming effect: 15 accu￾126 racy contrasts remain reliable (Appendix B).
127 3.2 The value readout distinguishes the
128 exception
129 We next compare readouts of the computed digit

the corresponding queried-variable readout. 141

162 3.3 Patching localizes the no-chain effect
163 For Llama-3.2-3B without CoT, replacing the
164 name’s activations with its neutral twin’s removes
165 52% of lure errors when all layers are patched
166 (Figure 2). A span-by-layer sweep places the
167 strongest effects at the defining equation in lay￾168 ers 0–7, where removal reaches 69% (Appendix J).
169 The neutral-word control also damages 22% of
170 correct answers. The patch identifies a region sen￾171 sitive to the name, but the disruption limits what
172 we can say about the computation it restores.
173 The Llama-3.1-8B controls do not distinguish a
174 name-specific cause: neutral and alternative-lure
175 patches remove errors similarly. Under a gold
176 CoT, injecting the misleading name into a neutral
177 run of the exception model moves 2.7% of answers
178 to the lure, while the word control changes 0.4% at
179 the same layer. These interventions test only the
180 selected models and positions; they do not estab￾181 lish a common causal mechanism across models.
182 4 Related work
183 Kudo et al. (2026) locate intermediate-value read￾184 outs during arithmetic CoT; our competing names
185 distinguish a computed value from a lexical cue.
186 Earlier Stroop studies test a single next token
(Wang, 2026; Hu et al., 2026). Faithfulness stud- 187
ies perturb prompts, chains or parameters (Turpin 188
et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025; 189
Tutek et al., 2025); we combine a known conflict- 190
ing cue with internal readouts. Positional copying 191
and output priors can also explain arithmetic errors 192
(Liu, 2026; Prabhakar et al., 2024); our copy con- 193
trol is reported in Appendix E. A companion code 194
study (Anonymous, 2026) examines value writes 195
in Python traces; this paper tests arithmetic lure 196
answers and their association with value-step read- 197
outs. 198
5 Conclusion 199
Writing a chain limits lure answers in almost ev- 200
ery tested arithmetic contrast. Across the probed 201
pairs, this reduction accompanies an accurately de- 202
codable value at the writing step; stating the value 203
in the chain alone is insufficient. In the exception, 204
the chain itself can write the lure and pass it to the 205
answer. 206
Limitations 207
The task uses short synthetic arithmetic chains and 208
English number words. It does not establish how 209
misleading identifiers affect real code. Identifier- 210
style names were tested at one level only (Ap- 211
pendix D). Models are at most 12B parameters 212
and receive raw completion prompts; instruction- 213
tuned models’ chat templates and long-form rea- 214
soning modes are untested. Demonstration choice 215
changes accuracy substantially, which is why ev- 216
ery behavioral claim must hold across three sets. 217
Linear probes reveal decodable information, not 218
necessarily the representation used to generate an 219
answer. Most internal measurements supply the 220
correct chain token by token. The small sample of 221
models’ own chains is illustrative (Appendix E). 222
The value-readout association holds across the 223
probed pairs but is unresolved within instances 224
and may reflect overall task competence. Patch- 225
ing changes mixed representations and can dam- 226
age correct answers, limiting the causal conclu- 227
sions. Finally, the chain repeats names and equa- 228
tions; the value-only format changes performance 229
and cannot be treated as an equivalent intervention 230
(Appendix H). 231

B Equivalence checks 397
Following preregistration Amendment 3, equiv- 398
alence requires a 90% cluster-bootstrap interval 399
wholly inside the ±2-point margin (two one- 400
sided 5% tests). We resample matched sets with 401
their demonstration-seed replicates together, using 402
4,000 bootstrap draws. Other behavioral intervals 403
remain at 95%. For CoT accuracy, 61 of 88 fa- 404
cilitation and interference contrasts meet the same 405
equivalence bound. Of the 15 reliable accuracy 406
contrasts, 5 are also equivalent within the margin 407
and 10 are not; a detectable effect can still be small. 408
The written-value control uses the same matched 409
pairs for its excess estimate and interval; the ex- 410
ception model’s estimate pools all three demon- 411
stration sets and clusters on matched set. 412

598 Our chain restates each equation before substitut￾599 ing into it, as Kudo et al.’s does. Their Tables 5–
600 6 also report a format that writes only the sub￾601 results, cup=5, pen=6. That format is the arith￾602 metic counterpart of a code trace, which writes a
603 variable’s value and never the expression that pro￾604 duced it, so we ran it on the panel at level 3 with
605 three demonstration sets, with two predictions reg￾606 istered in advance for both Llama models: that
607 the written-lure excess at the intermediate’s value
step would exceed the 2-point margin, and that the 608
value would still be decodable there (probe accu- 609
racy at least 0.85), making any failure one of read- 610
out. 611
Both predictions fail as registered. Llama-3.1- 612
8B writes the lure at the intermediate’s value step 613
+0.15 points above its twin, inside the margin. 614
Llama-3.2-3B is at +9.20, which passes our claim 615
rule, but across the three demonstration sets that is 616
+1.7 / +24.2 / +1.7, so one set carries most of it. 617
More informative is what the probes say. At the 618
step that writes the value, the true value is decod- 619
able at 0.81 and 0.59 for Llama-3.1-8B and at 0.61 620
and 0.79 for Llama-3.2-3B, against 1.00 and 0.99 621
for the same model under the full chain. Neutral 622
accuracy falls with it, to 54% and 31%. 623
